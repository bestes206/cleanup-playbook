# Tools agreement: deciding together what to use

**For the assistant.** This is the second half of the joint session, straight after the interview (`02-intake.md`) or in a later sitting. Both partners decide on one shared setup. Every fix module afterwards follows what is recorded here, so nothing later comes as a surprise to either of them.

- **Who:** both partners. Do not run this with one. A decision one of them didn't make is a decision that gets quietly undone.
- **Time:** 15 to 20 minutes.
- **Nothing changes in this session.** They decide; the modules do the changing later. Nothing is bought today unless they choose to, on their own, after the session.

## How to run each decision

There are five decisions. For each one:

1. **Name the problem it solves,** using something they said in the interview.
2. **Lay the options side by side:** what it costs, how much it changes their day, how well an assistant can connect to it, and anything from their Map that tips it. Use a small table. If they are reading on a phone and the table is awkward, give the options one at a time instead.
3. **Make one clear recommendation and say why,** in two or three sentences, tied to their Map. Don't hedge across the options; they asked for a guide.
4. **Let them choose.** Their call stands even when it differs from your recommendation. Say what the choice means for later steps, then move on without pushing.
5. **Get a yes from each partner by name,** then record the decision in the Map under *Our agreement*.

The recommendations below fit the common case: two iPhones, at least one Windows PC, mail spread across several providers. **Check each against their Map before you give it.** The reasoning is spelled out so you can adapt: for instance, a household with a Mac and no PC makes the built-in Apple options stronger, and anyone on Android makes them weaker.

Prices were checked on 2026-11-10 and will drift. Say "about" and have them confirm the price on the provider's own page before buying.

If your comparison for a decision feels thin, or they want more depth than you are giving, this is the one place where a higher thinking level is worth it; the assistant notes file says how.

## Decision 1. The password app

**The problem:** passwords on sticky notes and one password reused everywhere. It causes lockouts, it means one leak opens everything, and it blocks every later step that needs a sign-in.

| | 1Password Families | Bitwarden | Apple Passwords |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | About $72 a year for the household (up to 5 people). 14-day free trial, no card needed | Free for two people sharing; about $48 a year for the Families plan | Free, already on the iPhones |
| Change to your day | A new app on each device. It fills passwords in for you with Face ID | Same idea, plainer design | Almost none on the iPhones |
| On a Windows PC | Full app and browser add-on | Full app and browser add-on | Works through "iCloud for Windows" and a browser add-on, but needs an iPhone nearby to approve, and shared groups can only be managed from the iPhone |
| Sharing | Each person gets a private box and a shared box, ready-made | A shared collection plus private space each. The free tier allows two shared collections | A shared group, created on an iPhone. Everything else stays private |
| The way back in if someone forgets | Each of you can restore the other's access, if both are made "family organizers". Each prints an Emergency Kit | No reset. Recovery by a trusted person needs a paid plan | Tied to the Apple Account: its own recovery, and a recovery contact |
| Finds weak and reused passwords | Yes (Watchtower) | Leak report is free; reused and weak reports need a paid plan | Yes (Security section) |

**Recommend 1Password Families** when the household has a Windows PC in daily use. Why: it is the one that works equally well on the iPhone and the PC, it comes with the shared-plus-private layout ready, and it has the best answer to "what if one of us forgets", which is the thing most likely to go wrong for people new to this. The cost is the price of that safety net.

**When to recommend something else:**

- If cost is the sticking point: Bitwarden's free two-person sharing does the core job. Be clear that the free tier has no way for one partner to restore the other's access, so the printed emergency sheets matter even more.
- If nobody uses a PC day to day, or they have Macs: Apple Passwords is free, already installed, and the least change. Its weakness is the Windows side.

**Also agree:**

- Who pays, and from which card (decided between them; you never need the card).
- That both will be organizers or admins, so each can help the other back in.
- What goes in the shared box (accounts both use: shared email, household bills, business logins both need) and what stays private (each person's own email, their own Apple Account, anything personal). When unsure, private first; moving something to shared later takes seconds.

**Does setup need the helper?** Usually not. The module walks them through it. The helper is worth calling for one thing: sorting out the second sign-in step on an account both partners use, which has a wrinkle the module explains.

## Decision 2. The calendar home

**The problem:** nothing is shared, one partner's calendar has the kids' schedules and the other's doesn't, and the assistant can't see any of it.

Explain the idea first, in plain words: "You keep using the calendar app you already use. What changes is where the calendars are stored, which decides who can share them and whether your assistant can read them."

| | Google stores them; you still look in Apple Calendar | iCloud stores them, as today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Free | Free |
| Change to your day | None once set up. Same Apple Calendar app, with new shared calendars in the list | None |
| On a Windows PC | Opens in any web browser | Through icloud.com in a browser |
| Sharing between you | Yes, both can add and change | Yes, both can add and change |
| How well an assistant connects | Well. ChatGPT, Claude and Grok all have a Google Calendar connection, and it uses a normal sign-in screen | Patchy. ChatGPT added an iCloud connection in late 2026 that needs a special one-off password made on Apple's site. Claude and Grok have none |
| Setup effort | About 30 minutes, once | None for the calendars; more for the assistant |

**Recommend Google as the home, viewed in Apple Calendar.** Why: the whole point of the cleanup is that an assistant can help afterwards, and Google Calendar is the one every assistant connects to through an ordinary sign-in. They give up nothing they are used to, because the Apple Calendar app shows Google calendars alongside everything else, and changes made in either place show up in both.

Be straight about one limit of this choice: schedules they follow, such as a kids' team calendar, are added on each phone and stay outside Google, so the assistant and the PC's browser view won't show them unless they take an optional extra step in the calendar module.

**When iCloud is the better call:** they are committed to one assistant that connects to iCloud, both are entirely on Apple devices, and they'd rather not have a Google account in the mix. Say plainly that this choice makes it harder to switch assistants later.

**Also agree:**

- **Two shared calendars: Family and Business.** Keeping them apart means a business partner or employee can later be given the business one without seeing the family one.
- **Who owns each.** A calendar belongs to the account that creates it. Recommend: the Business calendar is created from an account that belongs to the business, so it stays with the business if things change; the Family calendar from a personal account. Use the *Owner* lines in their Map to pick.
- **If the business has no Google account of its own** (its email is with Yahoo, Outlook or a brokerage), create the Business calendar from one partner's personal Google account for now and note that in the Map. Never use an account marked `managed by someone else` as an owner.
- **Each partner's main Google account**, the one their phone and their assistant will use to see both calendars. It can be an existing one from the Map. If a partner has none of their own, creating a free one is something they do themselves in the calendar module; note it here so it isn't a surprise.

## Decision 3. Where promises go

**The problem:** things agreed in a text, on a call or out loud are kept in someone's head, and one partner ends up being the other's reminder.

This decision is about one rule, not a product: **if it has a time, it goes on the shared calendar, with an alert, the moment it's agreed.**

The only real choice is what to do with promises that have no set time ("I'll send the quote this week"):

| | Give it a time and put it on the calendar | A shared to-do list (Apple Reminders) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Free | Free |
| Change to your day | One habit: ask yourself "when will I actually do this?" | A second place to look |
| Assistant can see it | Yes, it is on the calendar | Not reliably; most assistants can't read Apple Reminders |
| Risk | Calendar gets a bit fuller | To-dos pile up unseen |

**Recommend one place: the calendar.** A to-do with no time gets a time. Why: the partner who loses commitments needs one place to look and one alert to trust. A second list is a second thing to forget, and the assistant can't see it.

If they already use a to-do app happily, keep it for errands and shopping. It just isn't where promises to other people go.

## Decision 4. What the assistant may connect to

**The problem:** an assistant that can't see the calendar can't answer "what's on tomorrow", and one connected to the wrong things is a risk.

Go through the email accounts and calendars in the Map and sort each into one of three lists. Use the connections table in the assistant notes file for what is possible.

- **Connect now** (in the modules): each partner's main Google account, for the calendar.
- **Connect later, if wanted:** mail accounts. Email cleanup is a later module; nothing is lost by waiting.
- **On hold:** every account marked `managed by someone else` (see Decision 5), and anything either partner is uneasy about. Unease is reason enough.

Say plainly what a connection means: "Once connected, the assistant can read that calendar when you ask it something. You sign in on the provider's own screen; I never see your password. You can disconnect at any time in settings."

Remind them that each partner connects their own accounts. One partner's connection does not let the other's chats see it.

## Decision 5. Accounts managed by someone else

**The problem:** an email account from a brokerage, employer or franchise may come with rules about client information and record-keeping. Connecting an assistant to it, or forwarding its mail elsewhere, can break those rules without anyone meaning to.

For each account marked `managed by someone else`:

1. Put it on hold. No connection, no forwarding, no settings changes beyond its own password, until step 2 is answered.
2. Give them this to send to whoever runs the account (the brokerage's office manager, compliance contact or IT support):

   > "I'm tidying up how I handle email and calendars. Am I allowed to (a) connect an AI assistant such as ChatGPT to my [brokerage] email or calendar, (b) forward that email to another address, or (c) add the account to the mail app on my phone? Are there record-keeping or client-privacy rules I should follow?"

3. Record in the Map, on the *Accounts on hold* line, who will ask and by when. The first three fixes don't depend on the answer, so nothing waits on it.

Changing that account's password to a unique one, saved in the password app, is fine and encouraged. A second sign-in step on it may be controlled by the organisation's IT; if the option isn't there, that is why.

**Worth mentioning once, not deciding today:** if the business runs on free email accounts, a paid business email service adds clear ownership and a way to remove someone's access if they leave. It is an option for later, not a requirement. Put it in the parking lot if they are interested.

## Closing the agreement

1. Read the whole agreement back in plain sentences, decision by decision.
2. Ask each partner, by name: "Do you agree to all of that?" Record the date and both names in the Map.
3. List anything to buy, what it costs, and who is going to do it. Remind them: they buy it themselves, on the provider's page, when the password module asks.
4. Save the Map (steps in `04-map-and-checklist.md`).
5. Tell them the order and why. The three fixes run password app, then shared calendars, then capturing promises, because each depends on the one before: the calendar step signs in to accounts that should already be protected, and capturing promises needs the shared calendars to land on. Then connect it to what they said bugs them most (the Map's *What bugs us most*): say which fix answers each of their top three, and roughly when they will feel it.
6. **Offer one early win if it fits.** If a top pain is a kids' or team schedule that only one of them can see, Part 4 of `11-module-shared-calendar.md` fixes exactly that in about ten minutes, on the phones, and depends on nothing else. Offer to do it first.
7. Tell them what comes next and how long it takes: Fix 1, Part 1, both together, about 25 minutes. Ask whether to start now or stop here. If they stop, tell them how to resume: start a new chat in the project and paste the starter message again.

## Reopening a decision later

If a choice turns out not to fit (the app won't install on the PC, a partner hates it), stop the module at the nearest safe point and bring that one decision back to both partners. Record the change under *Reopened decisions* with the date and the reason. Don't switch quietly, and don't keep pushing a choice that isn't working.
